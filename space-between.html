The Space Between: Hush

Interactive game concept · Interaction design · Narrative game design

The Space Between: Hush was developed as part of MIX333 – Computer Game Narrative, a master’s course at the University of Bergen focused on the relationship between game design, interaction and narrative. As part of the course, we analysed games and genres before developing our own narrative game concept. Our project explored how interaction, pacing and narrative could communicate emotional overwhelm and create an experience that encourages the player to slow down.

Design challenge

How can a game teach players to slow down rather than simply telling them to?

We wanted to explore how pacing and interaction could communicate emotional overwhelm without relying on explicit instructions. In Hush, the player's actions directly affect the stability of the game world, making slowing down something the player discovers through play.

The concept

Hush is the child's imaginary friend

The player takes the role of a child's imaginary friend, navigating an inner emotional world shaped by events in the child's everyday life. Emotional events in the outer world create shockwaves that affect the environment, while the player's pace determines how stable the world remains.

Core interaction

Fast vs Slow — environmental feedback

FAST
  • Storms become stronger
  • Cracks spread across the terrain
  • Landscape fragments and navigation becomes harder
SLOW
  • Storms calm
  • Terrain reconnects
  • Safe zones appear and navigation eases
Visual concept: These images were generated with Google Gemini to visualise how the environment could change between fast and slow player movement. They represent the intended visual direction rather than final game assets.

The volatility system is hidden — there is no stability meter or explicit indication of how the player is performing. Instead, the environment acts as feedback. Moving too quickly causes the world to become increasingly unstable, with stronger storms, spreading cracks and fragmented terrain. Slowing down has the opposite effect: the storm calms, the terrain reconnects and safe areas gradually appear. This makes the player's pace an important part of the interaction. Rather than being told to slow down, players learn through the changing environment that careful and deliberate movement creates a more stable world.

Player experience

Discovering care through interaction

Players gradually discover that their movement influences the world. Moving too quickly increases instability, while slowing down helps the environment recover. Rather than using explicit instructions, the game teaches players through environmental feedback, encouraging careful and deliberate movement.

Design takeaways

Key lessons

  • Interaction can communicate emotions: Rather than explaining emotional overwhelm through dialogue, we explored how pacing and environmental changes could make the player experience it.
  • Systems can teach without explicit instruction: The volatility mechanic was designed to let players discover the relationship between their movement and the stability of the world.
  • Concept development comes before implementation The project showed the value of defining the core interaction and player experience before moving toward a full game prototype..
  • Dependence → independence (progression through pacing): Players learn to rely on their own judgment and pacing to navigate the world effectively.

The mechanic is simple: rushing makes the world harder. Slowing down gives it space to recover.
